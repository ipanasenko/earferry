Walk through Amsterdam Central Station toward the river and the building suddenly opens onto water. Bikes gather, pedestrians adjust their direction, and a broad white-and-blue ferry approaches the landing. There is no ticket barrier. When the ramp settles, one small piece of the city flows aboard.

The water is the IJ, separating the historic centre from Amsterdam North. Ferries have connected these banks in different forms for centuries. Today, several municipal routes carry pedestrians, bicycles, scooters, and other light traffic across it. The busiest short crossings run frequently and cost passengers nothing.

That absence of tickets changes the experience. Boarding does not feel like the beginning of a special excursion. It feels like continuing along the street. A cyclist rolls from pavement to deck, waits a few minutes among commuters and visitors, then rolls onto pavement again on the other side.

Yet the ordinary journey creates a pause. Central Station recedes. Towers, cranes, new neighbourhoods, and passing vessels rearrange themselves across the horizon. Wind replaces traffic noise for a moment. Even people who make the crossing every day briefly share the same outward-facing room.

Amsterdam North was long shaped by shipbuilding and industry. As those areas changed, the ferries became even more important links to housing, workplaces, cultural venues, and public space. Routes and frequencies respond to that growing movement, while larger plans for bridges and tunnels repeatedly raise questions about ships, cyclists, cost, and the width of the IJ.

The ferry solves the problem in a wonderfully literal way. Instead of forcing the city around or beneath the water, it repeatedly carries a small section of the city across the surface.

That may be why these crossings feel memorable despite being so short. They are practical infrastructure with the view left in. For a few minutes, commuting and sightseeing become exactly the same activity.

Then the gates open, the deck empties, and the ferry turns around to collect another temporary street.
